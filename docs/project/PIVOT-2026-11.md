## 1. Data rights, first

### 1.1 Why this comes first

The collector's backbone is InvestorGain's private, undocumented JSON API: the calendar (`list-read`), the ~280-field
record behind every row and sheet (`ipo-detail-read`), the GMP (331), the whole evidence base since 2022 (377, 566), the

for BSE-only SMEs and the only source of application counts. Neither offers a licence, an API product or a price. A
hobby desk reading them for one person is the ordinary use of a public website. A company charging readers for a page
built on them is a different thing, and the day money changes hands is the day the question stops being theoretical.
Everything in sections 2 and 3 (what the lawyer is asked, what the platform stores) depends on what the product is
allowed to show, so this section is first.

### 1.4 Rule 1 on the table

Rule 1 — *the pipeline fetches structured data and renders it; it never reads documents* — was written on 18 Sep 2026
after an attempt to OCR anchor letters inside a run. It was a hobby-desk rule and a good one. The product reopens it,
because the RHP is both the licensing exit and the moat.

| Keep rule 1 as written | Amend it |
|---|---|
| Deterministic runs, no hallucinated digit, a gate that can refuse. Every lesson since September (the empty response, the CDN, the bare `env:`) was caught *because* the pipeline is simple | The one thing a parser cannot do and a model can — the reservation clause, the offer structure, the objects table — is exactly the thing a paid reader wants and InvestorGain was supplying |
| Documents are slow, big and scanned; a run that reads a 400-page PDF is a run that hangs (17 Sep's lesson) | §3's Shape C never puts a document in a *run*: an extractor on `workflow_dispatch`, outputs as reviewed files with page citations, the collector reads those files as data. The daily path stays document-free |
| Research by pull request kept a human between a document and the page | The same human stays: the extractor opens a draft PR; nothing lands unreviewed. Rule 3 keeps the model from writing a verdict (it fills fields; a regex refuses a digit in prose) |
| Cost and error compound at 220 issues a year | $300–700 a year and ~95% accuracy, with every field citing a page the reviewer can open — cheaper and more checkable than a hand-written sheet |

The amendment that keeps the reason for the rule and drops its blanket form:

> *The daily path never reads documents. A document becomes data only through the extractor: run by hand, output as
> fields with page citations, reviewed and merged as a file the collector then reads like any other. The page renders
> fields; it never parses sentences.*

## 2. Legal shape

### 2.1 The regulatory line: research analyst / investment adviser

Rule 3 — *evidence and both sides, never a verdict; every rate with n and a Wilson interval; allotment odds a lower
bound* — stays, and becomes the regulatory posture rather than a taste. The question for counsel is whether the page,
as it is and as a paid product, falls under either of SEBI's two definitions. This note cannot answer it; it can say
where the line runs and which features sit on which side.

**What the regulations say** (secondary sources read 13 Nov 2026; the consolidated text on sebi.gov.in is the
authority — verify every clause with counsel):

- *SEBI (Research Analysts) Regulations, 2014*, amended December 2024, with guidelines of 8 Jan 2025 and FAQs of 23 Jul
  2025. A **research analyst** is a person primarily responsible for a research report, a buy / sell / hold
  recommendation, a price target, **or an opinion concerning a public offer** — that last limb names IPOs directly. A
  **research report** is a written or electronic communication with research analysis or a recommendation on securities
  or a public offer that provides a basis for an investment decision. **Excluded**: general market commentary,
  discussion of trends and conditions, **statistical summaries of companies' financial data**, broad-based indices,
  technical analysis of a sector or index, periodic client statements. Journalists at media houses are exempt.
  Registration is with SEBI through BSE as the administration body (RAASB). Since the 2024 amendment: a deposit with a
  bank in lieu of net worth, scaled by client count (₹1 lakh up to 150 clients, rising to ₹10 lakh above 1,000 — verify);
  a NISM Series XV certificate for the analyst (the principal officer, for a company); a fee cap of ₹1,51,000 a year per
  family for individual and HUF clients (not a constraint at subscription prices); five-year records of every
  recommendation and its rationale; disclosures on every report; a compliance officer; disclosure when AI tools are used
  in research — relevant to the extractor (D1.4) whatever the answer.
- *SEBI (Investment Advisers) Regulations, 2013*: **investment advice** is advice on investing in, buying or selling
  securities, including financial planning, given for consideration; advice through newspapers, magazines, electronic or
  broadcast media **widely available to the public** is excluded. A subscription page is to subscribers; whether that is
  "widely available" is a question counsel answers, not this note.
- *Context counsel will raise*: SEBI's 2024 rules barring regulated entities (brokers, exchanges) from associating with
  unregistered persons who give advice or recommendations — so the registration question also decides whether a broker
  can ever partner with or distribute the desk; SEBI's press release 32/2026 (17 Jun 2026) warning investors off
  unauthorised pre-IPO and grey-market platforms (the desk publishes GMP, it does not trade it — the distinction to keep
  bright, D1.3); the "when-listed" segment, still in principle as of the last reports found.

**Both sides, feature by feature**, is section 2.3. The short version: the evidence bands, the Scoreboard's history and
the calendar read as *statistical summaries* and *data*; the expected value per application, "issues like this one" and
anything that names a specific open issue beside a computed figure read closer to *analysis providing a basis for an
investment decision*; and three labels on today's page are shaped like recommendations whatever the figure under them —
**"Open calls"** (Scoreboard), the **"hold-or-sell meter"** (Today, Board, Scoreboard) and the brief's own phrase "Hari's
real edge". Renaming costs nothing and removes the easiest exhibit against the desk (D2.4).

### 2.5 Terms, disclaimer, refunds, brand

Today the page carries one footer line ("…not investment advice. Nothing here places orders.") and two subtitles
("analysis, not advice"). A paid page needs, before the first rupee: **Terms of Use** (the subscription, acceptable use,
no advice, limitation of liability, governing law), the **Privacy Notice** (2.4), a **Disclaimer** that says what the
desk is and is not (not registered with SEBI as RA or IA — or registered, per D2.3; the grey market is unregulated and
GMP is a third-party relay; past bands are not forecasts; data sources named with their delays), a **Cancellation and
Refund policy** (the gateway and the E-Commerce Rules both want one; RBI's e-mandate rules for recurring card and UPI
debits apply to a monthly plan — pre-debit notice, the first debit with authentication — the gateway handles the
mechanics), and the **source attributions** on the page itself (exchange data "as published by NSE / BSE", SEBI, and
whichever GMP publisher D1.3 keeps).

**Brand.** IPODESK is descriptive ("IPO" + "desk"); the Trade Marks Act refuses marks that merely describe the service
(s. 9) unless they have acquired distinctiveness, so an IPODESK filing in class 36 (financial information) and 42
(software as a service) may draw an objection — the reason Hari's "maybe rebrand better" is also the lawyer's instinct.
A distinctive coined name files cleanly (government fee ₹4,500 a class for an individual or a recognised start-up,
₹9,000 otherwise; an attorney ₹3–8k a class). The company's legal name need not be the brand, so the entity can be
filed before the name is chosen. The page is name-light (the manifest, the title, the digest's first line, the
domain); a rebrand before launch is a grep, not a project — but it is a visible change and gets a mockup first.

## 6. Product and market

### 6.1 The customer

Beachhead: **the regular retail applicant** — applies to most mainboard IPOs at one lot (about ₹15,000), checks GMP and
the book several times a day while an issue is open, reads allotment odds as a lottery, forgets record dates, and keeps
money blocked in overlapping issues. Second, the segment Hari named: **affluent families that discuss and apply
together** from two to four demat accounts — same needs, times four, plus one person who keeps the family's book.
Later: sNII / bNII applicants (₹2–10 lakh an application; the NII book by the hour, odds by count); shareholder-quota
hunters (the desk's unique screen; a niche). **Not targeted**: SME traders — the segment rule 3 fits worst, and the one
SEBI watches hardest.

| | Regular retail applicant | Family desk (2–4 accounts) |
|---|---|---|
| Today's tools | InvestorGain / Chittorgarh for GMP and the book, the broker app to apply, a WhatsApp group for opinions, a notes app for dates | The same, plus a spreadsheet one person keeps |
| The job | Decide which issues to apply to, when, from which account; know when money comes back; not miss a quota | The same across accounts, and a record of who applied where |
| What the desk gives that they do not have | Every signal against its own track record with n and an interval; EV per application; the capital timeline; the quota planner; the pre-open card; anchors with records | All of that, plus one Book with an account field |
| The paid trigger | The five-minute book during bidding, the pre-open card on listing day, alerts, the Book | The family plan: N account books under one subscription |

### 6.2 The value, in Hari's words

Three things the desk did for him that a stranger would pay for: it changed which issues he applied to (EV and the
bands); it caught a shareholder quota in time (the planner's record date); it changed listing-day decisions (the pre-open
card against the day-one bands). Those are the three promises of the product, and each is a statistic or a tool, not a
tip: *"see what this signal was worth before, decide yourself"*, *"never miss a record date"*, *"know at 9:45 how it
is opening"*. Rule 3 is the fourth promise, and the one that sells trust: the page never tells a reader to apply.

### 6.6 Unit economics to ₹5 lakh a month

Prices are GST-inclusive as Indian consumer prices are; the company nets price ÷ 1.18, less the gateway's ~2% plus GST
on the fee. Payers needed for ₹5 lakh of monthly *billings* (annual plans counted monthly):

| Tier price | Nets per payer | Payers for ₹5 lakh |
|---|---|---|
| ₹299 | ~₹247 | ~1,670 |
| ₹799 | ~₹660 | ~630 |
| ₹1,999 | ~₹1,650 | ~250 |
| Blended ₹600 (Desk + Family mix, annual discounts) | ~₹495 | ~830 |

Reaching ~700 payers in twelve months is ~60 net new a month. Freemium products convert 2–5% of free sign-ups to
paid; founders convert far better; nobody knows this product's rate yet. At 5% the year needs ~14,000 free sign-ups,
~1,200 a month. With ₹25–50k a month of ads at ₹50–150 per free sign-up (unknown until measured) that is 300–1,000 a
month from paid channels, the rest organic (the digest, the Telegram community, posts, referrals). **So the ₹5 lakh
goal is reachable if two numbers come in: free-to-paid above ~5% and a sign-up under ~₹100.** Both are measured in the
first ninety days; the plan is re-cut on them, not on hope. At ₹299 alone the goal needs 1,700 payers and is a
different, larger funnel; the Family tier and the ₹799 band are what make the arithmetic work.
